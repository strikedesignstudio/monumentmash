Monument Mash Up is an interactive website, where the statues on Deptford Town Hall are re-imagined to challenge the legacies of colonisation and enslavement they signify. Developed through a unique workshop series including art, architecture, design, and theories drawn from the Black Radical Tradition, young people from Lewisham collaborated with artist Dele Adeyemo, community researchers from Deptford People’s Heritage Museum and academics from Goldsmiths BA Curating to generate 3D models. Drawing from practices of Black artistry, and community-led architecture and curating, these models bridge the colonial past and their experiences of Deptford today.

Deptford Town Hall is a Grade II listed building built in 1905 and acquired by Goldsmiths in 2000. The statues on its facade represent a colonial history that links to Transatlantic enslavement, plantation profiteering and racial injustice. In 2019, Goldsmiths Anti-Racist Action (GARA) held a 137-day sit-in occupation of the building, protesting inaction on racial injustice at the College. The occupation was brought to a close on 27 July 2019 after the College management and the campaigners established a mutually-agreed statement of commitments. One of these commitments was to allow greater community access and engagement with Deptford Town Hall. The occupation is an inspiration for local young people fighting for changes in the area, and it is in this legacy that Monumental Mash Up was born.

Part of the Deptford People’s Heritage Museum / Goldsmiths’ The Monument is the Struggle, this project attempts to answer the question what comes after the monument? Controversies over statues in the public realm often focus more on symbolic dimensions of the past and less on the persistence of dispossession in the present. We understand the monument to be a remnant of something more collective and relational, something that can be used to both contest problematic histories and generate resistant futures.

ABOUT THE WORKSHOPS

Through a unique workshop series including art, architecture, design, and theories drawn from the Black Radical Tradition, young people from Lewisham have come together to develop responses to the Deptford Town Hall statues working with artist Dele Adeyemo, community researchers from Deptford People’s Heritage Museum and staff from Goldsmiths BA Curating. Over five days in August 2025 the group worked through the implications of Deptford’s colonial history, its links to Transatlantic enslavement and to histories of Black-led resistance. Working with practices of Black artistry, and community-led architecture and curating, they were encouraged to consider their experiences of Deptford today.

Young people tested ways of working with objects important to their lives, communities and families and considered how histories interact with their present realities in South London. Through this process, one of their first creations was a table exhibition where they curated elements relevant to them and to the discussions being held:

As an outcome of the project, young people made their own 3D works in clay, considering how their experiences could be mapped through and across individual and collective worlds and onto the history and architecture of Deptford Town Hall. They then wrote and recorded spoken responses. All of these elements - their 3D objects, voices and architectural renderings of the Town Hall - are now reunited here. This website de-centres the current Deptford Town Hall and re-imagines its statues as a series of layered, living, interactive, polyvocal and complex relationships through which the words, images, objects and soundscapes of local young people and their communities can play a prominent role.

Living Lewisham Legends: Evodie Koke

“The microphone represents the two artists who inspired this statue - Showkey and Young Adz, both from Lewisham. The crown has double meaning, it creates connotations of them being legends, and serves as a potential Lewisham logo. ”

The arrival: Sabir Rooble

"Statue of a pirate ship with a fist and shackles: the colonial ship represents the arrival of the colonisers who enslaved indigenous people/people of colour. The shackles have two sides, the right side is chained and symbolises the people who were being enslaved and having their entire lives taken away from them, while the left side is broken, commemorating freedom and the autonomy over their own lives.

Further down, it’s possible to see an island of enslaved people being put to work. Within this island, there’s a whip that symbolises white supremacy and control over black people, but towards the end of the arrival, there is a fist symbolising freedom and black power.”

Crack an Egg: Dominic Wade

“The statue represents my own physical and mental scars from my childhood.”

Mini me: Rasheed Ebanks-Olanigan

“The wolf represents my position as a leader of his pack. The trophy references the sacrifices I have had to make, and the self-portrait shows how proud I am to be myself.”

The Black Unbreakable Kaiser: Marvin Gordon

“The work is called the Black Unbreakable Kaiser because of the story behind it, about a Black boy who grew up in care and had to face a lot of obstacles. Statistics say that Black boys who experience this become unsuccessful. These statistics are dehumanising, but I challenge this narrative by being a superhero - and having a black comb used as a weapon.”

Fight Against The Leech: Jermaine Johnson

“This statue is based on my poem about a community standing up against history erasure. The fist represents community and power, while the leech is meant to represent white people stealing and thriving off of black culture”

Let Down: Jessica Johnson

“The butterfly symbolises that life is not yet over, and it’s semi-colon shape refers to my mental health journey. Additionally, it links to my audio poem about struggle and how black people have to work twice as hard to get as far as their white counterparts, especially since Black mental health has been seen as insignificant in comparison to white mental health”.

Everlasting: Hannah Lewis

“The half finished skull represents my struggle against never-ending racism. The silenced mouth and no eyes reference the blindness to injustices that Black people face, and the lack of organs show their dehumanisation. It connects to my poem ‘Apart Of Me’, which talks about the parts of themselves that people leave behind when migrating from one place to another”.

Death Canoe: Henry Chinwendu

“The canoe acts as the vessel used during the transatlantic slave trade. The skull represents the death of people taken into slavery - both metaphorically and physically. The spheres show the great number of individuals that were transported at a time, even in crammed conditions. One of the chains represents the lack of freedom faced by enslaved people, however, next to it, the chain with a side cracked references their slow but certain liberation.”

The Stolen Prince: Oluwasemilore Olaniyan

“This is a Nigerian piece, with tribal marks that represent my Nigerian descent, and predominant features that represent black beauty. The figure is a symbol for black people as a whole and was inspired by a Benin Bronze found in the 19th century.”

Monument Mash Up is a collaboration between artist Dele Adeyemo, Deptford People’s Heritage Museum, a Black-led, community museum that develops programmes about histories of enslavement, colonisation and resistance in the local area, Goldsmiths BA Curating, and local young people.

Dr. Dele Adeyemo is a Scottish / Nigerian artist, architect, and critical urban theorist based in London and Lagos. In his creative practice, research and pedagogy he interrogates the racialising processes embedded in the production of space, often using participatory processes working with artists and communities. He has a doctorate from Goldsmiths, University of London, and has taught in schools of architecture around the world including running an Architecture Design Studio at the Royal College of Art in London for three years. Dele will lead the creation of the virtual reality model of Deptford Town Hall. https://www.deleadeyemo.com/

Deptford People’s Heritage Museum is a museum without walls founded by community organisers, a stone's throw from the Deptford Docks in London - currently the contested site of a major real estate development, and historical point of departure for ships associated with the violence and de-humanisation of the Transatlantic Slave Trade, East India Company and the plantation system. Developed with community groups including a food bank, women’s support collectives, health and racial justice advocates and other locally led initiatives, the Museum ‘collection’ includes maps, walks, study groups and objects narrated by people who live and work in the surrounding area, and who negotiate the diasporic legacies of the UKs colonial history in their everyday lives. Together, we trace histories and links to and from Deptford, tell the stories of local resistance and struggles for freedom, and plot these histories in relation to contemporary issues facing our community and others around the world. The museum is governed by a “village ethos”, involving current and ancestral voices in its ongoing process of museum-making excavating relationships between the past, present and future. Monumental Mash Up is part of our project the Monument is the Struggle, developed with Goldsmiths Department of Visual Cultures Dr Janna Graham and Dr Jorella Andrews. https://dphm.org/about
